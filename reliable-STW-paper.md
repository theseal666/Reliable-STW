# Diagnosing and Correcting Speed-Through-Water Error on a Foil-Limited, High-Righting-Moment Racing Sailboat in a Current-Dominated, Variable-Conductivity Sailing Area

## Abstract

Speed through water (STW), as measured by a paddlewheel log integrated into a B&G H5000 processor, is a foundational input to a racing sailboat's derived performance data — true wind, target speeds, VMG, and by extension tactical and trim decisions. On the vessel discussed here, STW is suspected — and on inspection, confirmed — to be systematically unreliable. This paper sets out the diagnostic reasoning developed over a working discussion: why the standard mitigations (manufacturer static compensation, a heel-based correction table, and GPS-referenced double-run calibration) each address only part of the error, why the residual error is dominated by leeway that is poorly correlated with heel on this hull form, and why the sailing area itself — strong, spatially and temporally variable current with unreliable tide-table prediction, and brackish, conductivity-variable water — defeats the usual GPS-based workarounds. It concludes with a proposed measurement architecture (direct two-axis flow sensing plus a joint, multi-variable calibration surface), a no-new-hardware fallback methodology (the two-tack leeway test), and a review of currently available hardware candidates with their known limitations.

## 1. Introduction

The vessel is an extreme, flat-bottomed, high-pointing design instrumented with a B&G H5000 processor and a paddlewheel STW sensor. There is strong operational evidence — inconsistent derived performance numbers, and decisions made downstream of them that later proved wrong — that the STW signal is not trustworthy. Two mitigations were already in place: the H5000's own rudimentary internal STW compensation, and the SignalK "speed and current" plugin, which applies a static speed/heel correction table. Neither was judged adequate by the crew, and the working hypothesis going in was that leeway — the sideways component of the boat's motion through the water — was the primary unmodeled variable.

The sailing area compounds the problem. Current is a constant, non-negligible factor; the area's islands, channels, and mixed bodies of water make tide-table prediction unreliable in practice; and the boat regularly transits between different water masses. This matters because, as this paper will argue, several of the standard techniques for correcting STW or estimating leeway implicitly assume current can either be ignored, looked up, or cancelled by symmetric maneuvers — assumptions that hold poorly here.

## 2. Problem statement

STW is not a directly fundamental quantity to the paddlewheel; the sensor reports an impeller rotation rate converted to a speed under the assumption that flow arrives parallel to the boat's centerline and that the local flow at the sensor represents free-stream flow. Both assumptions are violated in normal sailing: flow arrives at a leeway angle whenever the boat generates lateral (side) force to balance the sail plan, and the sensor sits within the hull's boundary layer, where flow is retarded and disturbed relative to the free stream. Static compensation tables — whether the H5000's internal model or the SignalK heel-table plugin — attempt to correct for this using heel angle as the explanatory variable. The crew's own logged data shows this is insufficient: at matched speed and heel, measured leeway varies substantially with sail set, trim mode, and sea state. Heel is therefore established empirically, not just theoretically, to be an inadequate proxy for the actual cause of the error on this hull.

## 3. Why this is hard: the underlying physics

### 3.1 Single-axis sensing and off-axis flow

A paddlewheel (and most conventional electromagnetic or ultrasonic speed logs) measures flow along essentially one axis, fixed relative to the hull. When water arrives at a nonzero leeway angle, the sensor is being asked to report a magnitude from a vector it cannot fully resolve. The relationship between true flow speed, leeway angle, and the sensor's raw reading is not necessarily a clean trigonometric (cosine) function — it depends on the specific sensor's geometry and its response to off-axis and separated flow — but in all cases the single-axis sensor is structurally blind to the transverse component that would let it self-correct.

### 3.2 Decoupling of heel and leeway on this hull form

On a conventional deep-keel monohull, heel and leeway are fairly tightly coupled because both arise from the same lateral force balance acting through a relatively fixed foil. On a flat-bottomed, high-righting-moment design of the kind under discussion, heel is generated substantially by righting moment (crew position, rig loads) while leeway is generated by how the flat sections and limited lateral plane resist side force — a relationship that is far more sensitive to angle of attack, boat speed, trim mode, and wave-induced changes in wetted shape. The practical consequence, observed directly in this program's own SignalK-logged data, is that heel is a weak predictor of leeway on this boat: the same heel and speed combination produces materially different leeway depending on sail set, trim mode, and sea state. Any correction scheme keyed on heel alone is therefore fitting the wrong (or at least insufficient) independent variable.

### 3.3 The current confound

Two of the most natural ways to obtain ground truth — comparing STW to GPS speed over ground (SOG), and comparing compass heading to GPS course over ground (COG) to infer leeway — both implicitly require knowledge of the current vector, because both compare a water-referenced quantity to a ground-referenced one. In an area where current is always present, spatially variable around islands and channels, and not well predicted by tide tables, neither comparison is trustworthy on its own. Critically, COG-minus-heading is not leeway; it is leeway plus current set, and the two cannot be separated by GPS and compass alone. This explains why naive attempts to "measure leeway with GPS" collapse back into the same current problem the exercise was meant to avoid.

Reciprocal-course ("double-run") calibration is a partial, valid workaround for the STW side of this problem: running a course and its reciprocal in quick succession, close enough in time and space that the current vector is approximately constant across both legs, allows the current to be cancelled arithmetically from the average of the two SOG readings, yielding a real STW ground truth without needing to know the current's value. This is a sound technique, and this program uses it. However, it establishes only the paddlewheel's static sensor error — offset and linearity — under whatever leeway condition prevailed during the test (typically motoring or a controlled reach, i.e. low leeway). It cannot, by construction, characterize the dynamic, sail-trim- and wave-dependent leeway error that dominates STW error under racing conditions. This is the most important finding of the diagnostic discussion: double-run calibration and leeway-driven error are not the same problem, and solving the first does not touch the second, which is consistent with this program's field observation that careful double-run work has done little to improve STW reliability under sail.

### 3.4 Boundary layer effects

Independent of leeway, conventional electromagnetic and paddlewheel sensors typically sample a small volume of water very close to the hull, within the boundary layer, where flow is both slowed by skin friction and disturbed by local turbulence — an effect that worsens with speed and sea state. This is a second, distinct source of error from the leeway/off-axis problem, though the two compound: boundary-layer disturbance changes with the same conditions (speed, waves, trim-driven wetted shape) that also drive leeway, making the two hard to disentangle from field data alone without a sensor designed to reach past the boundary layer.

### 3.5 Water conductivity as an electromagnetic-sensor-specific confound

Electromagnetic log sensors (as opposed to paddlewheels) operate on Faraday's law of induction: a magnetic field is induced, water passing through it generates a voltage proportional to conductivity and velocity, and electrodes measure that voltage. The induced signal's magnitude therefore scales with the water's electrical conductivity, which is high and roughly stable in open ocean saltwater, much lower in freshwater, and variable — potentially within a single outing — in brackish or mixed water, exactly the condition of a sailing area with islands, channels, and multiple connected bodies of water. An electromagnetic sensor without explicit conductivity/salinity compensation risks introducing a new systematic error (apparent sensitivity drift as the boat moves between water masses) even if it otherwise solves the leeway and boundary-layer problems. This is a design requirement to verify explicitly for any electromagnetic-sensor candidate considered for this program, not an incidental detail.

## 4. Methods evaluated

### 4.1 Reciprocal-course calibration (in use)

Cancels current, isolates paddlewheel sensor offset/linearity. Valuable and retained, but scoped correctly per §3.3: it does not characterize leeway-driven error and should not be expected to.

### 4.2 Heel-based static compensation (H5000 native; SignalK "speed and current" plugin)

Both apply a fixed heel-to-correction table. Empirically shown, via this program's own logged comparisons, to leave large unexplained variance because heel alone does not determine leeway on this hull form (§3.2). Retained only as a weak first-order correction, not a solution.

### 4.3 The two-tack leeway test (proposed, no new hardware required)

A direct analog of the reciprocal-course technique, applied to leeway instead of speed. Two tacks (port and starboard) are sailed at matched true wind angle, boat speed, heel, and trim mode, in close succession so that the current vector is approximately constant across both — the same assumption that underpins the reciprocal-course test. In the absence of leeway, the two ground tracks, referenced to the true wind direction, would be symmetric; the asymmetry between them isolates leeway without requiring the current to be known. Repeated across the boat's actual sail inventory, trim modes, and representative sea states, this generates a condition-tagged empirical leeway dataset far richer than a single heel curve, and can be used to fit a real multi-variable correction model even before any dedicated leeway sensor is fitted. This is recommended as a standing practice independent of any hardware decision.

## 5. Proposed measurement and calibration architecture

The diagnostic discussion converged on a specific target architecture, in three parts:

1. **Direct two-axis flow sensing.** A sensor that measures both the longitudinal and transverse (leeway-axis) components of flow at the hull, so that leeway is sensed rather than inferred from GPS, removing the current confound entirely for this measurement.
2. **Real-time correction of the raw longitudinal reading using the measured leeway angle**, using an empirically derived (not assumed-cosine) response function, since off-axis sensor response is geometry-specific.
3. **A joint, multi-variable calibration surface** — raw sensor output as a function of true STW, leeway angle, and possibly heel (for housing/mounting effects) — fitted together rather than as two independently applied, sequential corrections. This matters because a sensor's off-axis sensitivity plausibly itself varies with speed (flow-separation and boundary-layer behavior are speed/Reynolds-number dependent), so treating "speed calibration" and "leeway calibration" as separable stages risks systematic residual error. A practical, tractable compromise is to establish baseline speed linearity in the lowest-leeway sailing condition achievable (flat water, near-zero heel), then fit the residual error against measured leeway (and heel) from real sailing data, with an explicit check afterward for any remaining speed/leeway interaction rather than assuming full separability.

## 6. Candidate hardware

Two electromagnetic, dual-axis sensor designs were identified as directly relevant; both are distinct products from different companies, not variants of the same underlying design.

**Airmar DX900+.** A currently sold, dual-axis electromagnetic sensor outputting a direct nautical leeway angle sentence (NLA) alongside conventional speed/log sentences over NMEA 0183, at up to 10 Hz, with a specified accuracy of ±0.1 kt below 10 kt and ±1% above. The underlying concept dates to a 2009 announcement ("DX900-EM") that did not ship for the better part of a decade, finally reaching market as the DX900+ around 2017. Field reports (marine electronics press, owner forums) describe real-world reliability and calibration difficulties — inconsistent readings across the speed range despite specified linearity, suspected interference on at least one carbon-hulled racing boat, and reported sensitivity to bottom paint. Its patent (US10,852,142 B2, assigned to Airmar Technology Corp) explicitly documents a variable-gain preamplifier stage designed to compensate for differing water salinity/conductivity between fresh and salt water — a specific, engineered answer to the §3.5 concern, present in a currently shipping product.

**Brickhouse Innovations (Larry Marsh; US10,416,187 B2).** A separate, smaller-scale development. Its principal technical differentiator is deliberately moving the sensing electrodes away from the hull surface and out of the boundary layer — even a modest quarter- to half-inch offset is claimed to meaningfully reduce boundary-layer contamination, with larger annular-coil variants sampling a substantially larger water volume further from the hull to average out local flow disturbance, directly targeting the §3.4 problem. It also features removable electrodes for in-water fouling maintenance. It was field-tested on Navy vessels in 2014–2015 and reads as a specialist or small-batch product rather than a mainstream retail item; current availability, pricing, and lead time were not established and should be confirmed directly with the company. Notably, the patent text reviewed makes no mention of conductivity or salinity compensation — an open question, not a confirmed absence, but one that must be resolved directly with the manufacturer given this program's brackish, conductivity-variable operating area, before any commitment.

A general caveat applies to any electromagnetic sensor considered, including a from-scratch build: conductivity/salinity compensation is a real design requirement in this sailing area, not an edge case, and its absence would risk trading one poorly understood systematic error (paddlewheel/leeway) for another (conductivity drift).

## 7. Open questions and next steps

- Confirm with Airmar, or other carbon-hulled DX900+ owners, whether carbon-hull interference is a material risk for this vessel.
- Obtain a direct answer from Brickhouse Innovations on salinity/conductivity compensation and validation in brackish or fresh conditions.
- Begin two-tack leeway testing (§4.3) across the sail inventory and trim modes this season, independent of any hardware decision, to begin building a real (STW, leeway, heel) dataset.
- Decide between buying the DX900+, pursuing a Brickhouse-style build or purchase, or continuing to refine the two-tack-test-derived correction table, based on the outcomes of the above.
- Once real two-axis data exists (from hardware or from two-tack testing), fit the joint calibration surface described in §5 rather than continuing to rely on a heel-only table.

## 8. Conclusion

The unreliability of STW on this vessel is best explained not as a single fault but as the superposition of at least three distinct effects — paddlewheel sensor offset/linearity (correctable by reciprocal-course calibration, already in use), leeway-driven off-axis flow error (poorly predicted by heel on this hull form, and not addressed by reciprocal-course calibration at all), and boundary-layer sampling error — compounded by an operating environment where the usual GPS-based shortcuts for either speed or leeway ground-truth are confounded by persistent, unpredictable current. The path to a trustworthy, low-maintenance STW signal runs through direct two-axis flow sensing (or, absent that, systematic two-tack leeway testing) feeding a jointly fitted, multi-variable calibration surface — not through a better single-variable heel table. Available two-axis hardware exists and is broadly the right architecture, but each current candidate carries its own unresolved risk (mechanical/hull-material interference for one, unconfirmed conductivity compensation for the other) that should be closed out before committing.
